Deriving and testing scaling rules.

01

The best scaling rule depends on the training setting.

Larger batches can increase training throughput, but retaining optimization performance is challenging and often requires adjusting hyperparameters. Throughout the years, multiple empirical and theoretical research programs have repeatedly investigated the correct "hyperparameter scaling rule" with respect to batch size. Can any of these prescriptions, or alternatives beyond them, can fulfill this promise:

Q1: Can a hyperparameter scaling rule consistently work in different training settings?

We summarize and derive scaling rules for MuonW-AdamW hybrid. We then compare them with a broad grid of candidate rules, combinatorially exhausting possible scaling rules for all hyperparameters, resulting in 216 possible scaling rules. Concretely, we train and tune a reference model in two settings: language modeling (following modded nanogpt speedrun track 3) at 128K tokens batch size, and CIFAR-5M image classification with ResNet with Group Norm at 256 image batch size. We then increase the batch size and test all the scaling rules. We will denote the new batch / reference batch as batch size ratio κ.

We found the promise does not hold:

  1. The optimal rule depends on the setting and target batch size.
  2. In language modeling, both the SDE invariant rule and convex bound minimization rule both fall behind the trivial baseline of keeping constant learning rate. The best performing rule at large batch size is prescribed by the Power Lines.
  3. On image classification, the bound minimization rule works the best instead.

Minimizing a bound

Square-root learning rate.

ηM′=κηM,μ′=μ,λM′=λM

Balances the initialization and smoothness terms in a stationarity bound, until its effective step-size cap becomes active.

Power Lines

Fixed learning rate.

ηM′=ηM,μ′=μ,λM′=κλM

Preserves the nominal parameter-averaging timescale at a fixed token budget. We extend the same identity to MuonW with fixed momentum.

Optimizer rankings across batch sizes.

02

Optimizer rankings can reverse, even after retuning.

Optimizer comparisons often use a single batch size. Yet changing the batch alters both gradient noise and the number of updates available within a fixed training budget. A poor scaling rule can also make an optimizer appear worse than it is. This raises our second question:

Q2: Do optimizer rankings persist across batch sizes after extensive retuning?

We compare Adam, Lion, Muon, SOAP, and Shampoo in the Modded-NanoGPT pretraining setting, from 128K to 2M tokens per update. Each run uses approximately 1.7 billion training tokens. We independently retune every optimizer at each batch size, so the comparison does not depend on a particular transfer rule. We test two ways of controlling weight norms. Decoupled weight decay shrinks weights at each update, while HyperBall keeps each hidden matrix’s Frobenius norm fixed.

Under HyperBall, Muon outperforms Shampoo at 128K, while Shampoo outperforms Muon at 2M.

Curvature and gradient noise.

03

Curvature and noise shape batch-size effects.

Sharp directions limit SGD’s learning rate. Newton removes that constraint but rescales the noise.

We study the noisy quadratic model (NQM) to isolate how curvature and gradient noise shape batch-size effects. An NQM pairs a quadratic loss with noisy gradient estimates. Larger batches reduce this noise. In the two-dimensional example below, one direction is flat and noisy, while the other is sharp and noiseless. We compare SGD with Newton’s method, which rescales each direction’s gradient by its curvature:

SGDnew position = position − learning rate × estimated slope

Newtonnew position = position − learning rate × estimated slope / curvature

We independently tune both learning rates to minimize expected loss after 4,096 processed samples. Increasing the batch reduces gradient noise, but also reduces the number of updates within this budget. The reference configuration shows:

  1. At batch 1, SGD reaches lower expected loss despite Newton’s curvature correction.
  2. At batch 4,096, only one update is available. Newton nearly removes the starting error in both directions, while SGD’s shared learning rate cannot.
  3. The crossover depends on curvature, noise, and the starting point. The same batch size can favor different methods in different optimization problems.

We also vary the sharp-direction curvature, flat-direction noise, and initial error. The winner map compares independently tuned expected losses at each batch and noise level. The paths and loss curves show one sampled run. The bars decompose expected loss after the 4,096-sample tuning budget into remaining starting error and noise contribution.

The 3D figure, adjustable trajectories, and live-loss curves end at that same 4K budget. With noise and a constant learning rate, the paths can still fluctuate around the minimum. This is a numerical illustration of the mechanism in Section 5, not the paper’s theorem construction or a proof that every landscape has a crossover.

For every sufficiently large sample budget, there exists a two-dimensional NQM on which optimally tuned SGD outperforms optimally tuned Newton at batch 1, but the ranking reverses when the whole budget forms one batch. The theorem holds both without momentum and with optimally tuned momentum. The displayed no-momentum playground is a separate numerical illustration. The construction establishes that directional differences in the bias–variance tradeoff can reverse optimizer rankings even after optimal tuning.

Directional scaling rules.

04

Higher curvature-to-noise ratios shift learning rates toward linear scaling.

In SignSGD, a noisy direction benefits from more reliable signs. A direction whose sign is already reliable mostly gets fewer updates.

A shared learning-rate rule applies the same batch-size exponent to every direction. Can directions with different noise levels favor different scaling rules? We study SignSGD with momentum on a one-dimensional noisy quadratic to isolate this dependence.

We vary gradient noise while holding curvature, initialization, momentum, and the 4,096-sample budget fixed. The curvature-to-noise ratio (CNR) is:

CNR=curvature2gradient-noise variance

Noise variance is measured for a single example. We independently tune the learning rate at each batch size and compare it with the rate at batch 1.

The tuned scaling changes with CNR:

  1. At CNR 0.001, the fitted learning-rate exponent is 0.588, close to square-root scaling.
  2. At CNR 0.03 and 1, the exponent rises to 0.794 and 0.904, moving toward linear scaling.

Can one exponent preserve both directions?

To explain this shift, we compare two directions with equal curvature and different CNRs. SignSGD’s learning rate sets the step length; noise determines how reliably the sign points toward the minimum. We apply a shared rule:

new learning rate = reference learning rate × batch ratioexponent

We define movement as expected displacement toward the minimum per processed sample, holding the parameter fixed and assuming stationary momentum. The chart divides this quantity by its batch-1 value, so 1× means the rule preserves movement.

The local calculation separates two regimes:

  1. When noise dominates, larger batches make signs more reliable. Preserving movement approaches square-root learning-rate scaling.
  2. When the sign is already reliable, larger batches mainly reduce the number of updates. Preserving movement approaches linear scaling.

One shared exponent leaves a mismatch between these directions. This calculation compares updates at a fixed point; the preceding experiment tunes loss over complete trajectories.

Where do these curves come from?

Throughout the blog, B is a batch size before scaling, B′ the scaled batch, and κ=B′B their ratio. η is a learning rate, μ a momentum coefficient, and α a learning-rate exponent. Both directions use w=1,μ=0.9.

Assume independent Gaussian gradient noise. Freeze the coordinate w and let the momentum distribution settle to its steady state; this is stationary momentum. Let μ be the momentum coefficient and κ=B′B the batch ratio. The expected sign is A(B)=erf(wCNR·B(1+μ)2(1−μ)). Relative movement per processed sample is κα−1A(κB)A(B). We use reference B=1. Noise-dominated response grows approximately as B; signal-dominated response saturates. Preserving this local movement motivates square-root or linear scaling in the respective limits. The approximation freezes the parameter and assumes stationary momentum; it does not guarantee full-trajectory invariance. See Section 5 and Appendix C of the paper.

The sharp-subspace intervention.

05

The sharpest 0.001% of directions remove up to 59.5% of the local penalty.

The quadratic analysis suggests that a batch-size change can affect directions differently. We test whether this sensitivity is concentrated in a small subspace during language-model pretraining.

We branch from checkpoints of a tuned 128K-token MuonW run and increase the batch size to 2M. Continuations from the same checkpoint share their starting state and training data. Each processes approximately 134 million additional tokens. We select sharp directions using a local curvature approximation after Muon’s preconditioning. The comparison changes only the matrix updates; the remaining parameters retain their original AdamW updates:

  • 128K control: Small-batch updates in every matrix direction.
  • Fully scaled to 2M: Large-batch updates in every matrix direction.
  • Top-k: Small-batch updates in the k sharpest directions; large-batch updates elsewhere.
  • Random 768: Small-batch updates in a random subspace of the same size as top-768.

The local branch penalty is the extra validation loss relative to the 128K control. Recovery is the share of the fully scaled penalty removed by retaining small-batch updates in the selected subspace:

penalty = branch loss − small-batch control loss

recovery = penalty removed / fully scaled penalty

We find that sharp directions carry much of the local batch-size penalty:

  1. Keeping the sharpest 768 directions, about 0.001% of the matrix-parameter space, removes up to 59.5% of the penalty across all measured checkpoints. At checkpoint 5,000, recovery is 59.3%.
  2. Recovery generally grows with the number of retained directions. The sharpest 16 remove up to 27%, though they do not reduce the penalty at checkpoint 2,000.
  3. A random 768-dimensional subspace changes the penalty by at most 3%. Selecting directions by sharpness matters more here than the subspace size alone.
  4. Recovery falls close to zero at the final two measured anchors, steps 11,000 and 12,000.

Protocol, controls, and what this does not establish

The sharp directions have the largest eigenvalues of a Muon-preconditioned Gauss–Newton approximation to the loss Hessian, a local curvature proxy. Branches continue for 1,024 base steps, except the 12K checkpoint, which runs for 992 steps to the end of training. The penalty subtracts its small-batch control’s final validation loss.

The held subspace stays fixed throughout the continuation. Both Muon copies receive the same stream of base-batch gradients. Every 16 steps, the large-batch copy uses their mean gradient and 16 times their mean learning rate. Matrix weight decay and auxiliary AdamW updates stay on the base clock. This is a diagnostic intervention, not an ordinary large-batch training run.

Endpoints use 10.5M held-out tokens. Most branches are single runs, so these percentages are descriptive measurements, not significance estimates. The experiment selects directions by curvature and does not measure the full direction-dependent noise covariance. Its effect largely disappears near the end of training. We therefore cannot infer a 59.5% improvement in final loss, total training cost, or wall-clock speed.

The intervention identifies directional sensitivity to batch scaling. Whether CNR predicts that sensitivity remains open: we select directions by curvature and do not measure their full noise covariance. These short continuations do not establish an improvement in final loss or end-to-end training cost.

Choose the optimizer and batch size together.

An optimizer ranking measured at one batch size need not persist at another, even after extensive retuning. Optimizer comparisons should use the batch sizes intended for training, with each configuration tuned independently.

The quadratic analysis shows how curvature and gradient noise can produce optimizer crossovers. The language-model intervention finds that a small set of sharp directions can account for much of the local batch-size penalty. Whether these directional differences yield a practical scaling rule or improve a complete training run remains open.
