Deriving and testing scaling rules.

01

The best scaling rule depends on the training setting.

216 rules in language modeling. 648 on CIFAR-5M. The best common prescription differs, and the language-model winner also changes with the target batch.

Larger batches can increase training throughput, but retaining optimization performance is challenging and often requires adjusting hyperparameters. Batch-size scaling rules prescribe these adjustments without requiring additional tuning. Different derivations yield conflicting prescriptions for the same optimizer. We study this question with MuonW–AdamW, which applies MuonW to hidden weight matrices and AdamW to the remaining parameters.

We derive two candidates by applying theoretical frameworks designed to preserve training dynamics via a stochastic differential equation (SDE) or minimize convergence bounds. Let κ=B′B denote the ratio of target to reference batch size. For Muon, ηM, λM, and μ denote the matrix learning rate, matrix weight decay, and momentum coefficient. Matching the SDE coefficients suggests linear learning-rate scaling and decay-preserving momentum. Minimizing a stationarity bound instead suggests square-root learning-rate scaling with fixed momentum. Both candidates keep matrix weight decay fixed. These derivations rely on different assumptions; neither is an exact invariance guarantee for the implemented optimizer.

Matching an SDE

Linear learning rate.

ηM′=κηM,μ′=μκ,λM′=λM

The idealized SDE calculation holds the coefficient mixing gradients and momentum fixed. MuonW ties this coefficient to momentum, so exact invariance is not guaranteed.
Minimizing a bound

Square-root learning rate.

ηM′=κηM,μ′=μ,λM′=λM

Balances the initialization and smoothness terms in a stationarity bound, until its effective step-size cap becomes active.
Power Lines

Fixed learning rate.

ηM′=ηM,μ′=μ,λM′=κλM

Preserves the nominal parameter-averaging timescale at a fixed token budget. We extend the same identity to MuonW with fixed momentum.

We test whether a single scaling rule transfers well across training settings and target batch sizes. For MuonW–AdamW, we evaluate all combinations of fixed, square-root, or linear scaling for the two learning-rate groups and each weight-decay coordinate, together with fixed or decay-preserving scaling for Muon momentum and Adam’s two moment coefficients. This gives 216 rules in language modeling, where auxiliary weight decay is held fixed. An additional sweep at 1M and 2M tokens found that scaling auxiliary AdamW weight decay offers little benefit. On CIFAR-5M, we test 648 rules, scaling matrix and auxiliary weight decays separately.

We transfer a tuned reference configuration to four larger batches in language modeling and six target batches in one-pass CIFAR-5M training with a GroupNorm ResNet. The reference batches are 128K tokens and 256 examples, respectively. Within each setting, all rules share the model, data budget, and schedule shape. The paper selects a common rule by its mean batch-size regret, the equally weighted mean validation-loss gap to the best tested grid rule at each batch. The figure below uses the lowest recorded loss at each batch across the original grid and the paper’s full-retuning results. Full retuning independently adjusts every hyperparameter at each batch by coordinate descent.

The best rule depends on the setting. The selected language-model rule keeps both learning rates fixed and scales matrix weight decay linearly with batch size. The selected CIFAR-5M rule instead scales both learning rates with the square root of batch size and keeps both weight decays fixed. The best common rules in both settings keep Muon momentum and Adam’s first-moment coefficient fixed. Thus, even for the same optimizer, the two settings favor different scaling prescriptions.

Within language modeling, the best scaling rule also changes across target batches. The best rules use square-root scaling for both learning rates at 256K and 512K tokens, but fixed learning rates and linear matrix weight-decay scaling at 1M and 2M. In the paper’s full-retuning comparison, the empirical LLM rule stays closest to full retuning, while both theoretical rules fall behind no scaling from 1M onward. On CIFAR-5M, the bound-minimization rule instead stays within 0.006 nats of full retuning.

Note: no scaling is a competitive baseline. Keeping every reference hyperparameter fixed ranks 12th out of 216 language-model rules by mean regret across 256K–2M. It ranks ahead of the grid versions of the paper’s bound-minimization and SDE recipes, which rank 22nd and 160th, respectively. If a scaling rule has not been validated in your setting, start with no scaling as a baseline.

Optimizer rankings across batch sizes.

02

Mediating the Muon and Shampoo debate.

Muon leads Shampoo at 128K under HyperBall; Shampoo wins at 2M under both norm controls. SOAP has the lowest loss at 128K.

Beyond how to adjust Muon’s hyperparameters, we ask whether batch size changes its performance relative to other optimizers. Adaptive optimizers differ in how they shape updates using minibatch estimates of quantities such as gradient second moments. Batch size controls the noise in these estimates, potentially changing which form of adaptation is most effective.

We follow the Modded-NanoGPT optimization benchmark and compare Adam, Lion, Muon, SOAP, and Shampoo from 128K to 2M tokens per update, holding the training budget at roughly 1.7 billion training tokens. Because scaling rules are unreliable, we perform extensive coordinate-descent tuning for each optimizer at each batch size. The compared optimizer acts on hidden matrices; embeddings, the output projection, and one-dimensional parameters use AdamW. We compare decoupled weight decay with HyperBall, which constrains each hidden matrix to a fixed Frobenius norm.

To understand how such crossovers can arise even after optimal tuning, we study a noisy quadratic model.

Curvature and gradient noise.

03

Curvature and noise shape batch-size effects.

A preconditioner changes both progress from the starting point and the noise left by stochastic updates. Batch size changes that tradeoff.

We study the noisy quadratic model (NQM) to isolate how curvature and gradient noise shape batch-size effects. Each coordinate has a positive curvature and a single-example gradient-noise variance. Its curvature-to-noise ratio (CNR) is curvature squared divided by single-example gradient-noise variance. A minibatch gradient adds independent Gaussian noise whose variance is divided by batch size. At a fixed sample budget, increasing the batch therefore reduces both the noise per update and the number of updates available.

We compare SGD with Newton’s method on a two-dimensional noisy quadratic. The two directions have different curvature-to-noise ratios because w1 is flat and noisy while w2 is sharp and noiseless. SGD uses one learning rate in both directions. Newton’s method divides each gradient coordinate by its curvature. At the same sufficiently small learning rate, SGD reduces initialization error more slowly in flatter directions, whereas Newton reduces it at the same rate in every direction while rescaling its noise by the inverse curvature. Batch size changes the balance between these effects.

Directional scaling rules.

04

Different curvature-to-noise ratios require different learning-rate scaling.

In SignSGD, a noisy direction benefits from more reliable signs. A direction whose sign is already reliable mostly gets fewer updates.

In the noisy quadratic setting, Muon on a 1 × 1 block approximately reduces to SignSGD with momentum because ideal orthogonalization is the sign operation. We examine SignSGD with momentum and no weight decay. On a one-dimensional noisy quadratic, we vary the noise variance while holding curvature, initialization, sample budget, and momentum fixed. We independently tune the learning rate at each batch size.

The curvature-to-noise ratio is squared curvature divided by single-example gradient-noise variance, CNRi=hi2ci. Increasing CNR shifts the tuned learning rates from approximately square-root toward linear scaling. The left panel shows the paper’s original one-dimensional experiment.

SignSGD takes a step of magnitude η in the direction determined by the sign of the momentum average. At a fixed parameter value, the stationary momentum average is Gaussian. When noise dominates, the magnitude of its expected sign grows approximately with the square root of batch size, and preserving displacement per processed sample motivates square-root learning-rate scaling. When signal dominates, its magnitude saturates at 1 and linear scaling compensates for fewer updates. The right panel evaluates this local movement criterion in two directions with different CNRs.

Where do these curves come from?

Throughout the blog, B is a batch size before scaling, B′ the scaled batch, and κ=B′B their ratio. η is a learning rate, μ a momentum coefficient, and α a learning-rate exponent. Both directions use w=1,μ=0.9.

Assume independent Gaussian gradient noise. Freeze the coordinate w and let the momentum distribution settle to its steady state; this is stationary momentum. Let μ be the momentum coefficient and κ=B′B the batch ratio. The expected sign is A(B)=erf(wCNR·B(1+μ)2(1−μ)). Relative movement per processed sample is κα−1A(κB)A(B). We use reference B=1. Noise-dominated response grows approximately as B; signal-dominated response saturates. Preserving this local movement motivates square-root or linear scaling in the respective limits. The approximation freezes the parameter and assumes stationary momentum; it does not guarantee full-trajectory invariance. The sharp-subspace intervention.

05

The sharpest 0.001% of directions remove up to 59.5% of the local penalty.

Keep a tiny selected subspace at small batch after a 16× batch increase. A random subspace of the same size changes the penalty by at most 3%.

Batch-size branching means copying a training checkpoint and continuing from it at a different batch size. All branches start from the same model and optimizer state, use the same data stream, and are compared after the same number of tokens. This measures the local cost of increasing the batch.

A critical batch size (CBS) marks where increasing the batch stops reducing the number of updates to a target loss proportionally. Directional CBS applies this idea to individual directions or subspaces: different directions may reach diminishing returns at different batches. Our intervention tests their sensitivity to a change from 128K to 2M; it does not estimate a separate CBS for each direction.

We branch from checkpoints of a tuned MuonW run at 128K tokens per step. Each continuation trains for 1,024 further base steps. The directional branch takes small-batch updates inside a fixed held subspace and large-batch updates outside it. Both Muon copies receive the same stream of 128K-token gradients. Every 16 steps, the large-batch copy uses their mean gradient and 16 times their mean learning rate. Matrix weight decay and auxiliary AdamW updates remain on the base clock.

The control keeps every matrix direction at 128K; the fully scaled branch moves them all to 2M. The local branch penalty is the branch’s final validation loss minus the control’s final validation loss from the same checkpoint.

We select directions with the largest eigenvalues of a Muon-preconditioned Gauss–Newton approximation to the loss Hessian, a local curvature proxy at the checkpoint. The held subspace stays fixed throughout the branch. As a baseline, we also hold a random 768-dimensional subspace. Keeping the base batch in only the sharpest 768 of roughly 85 million hidden-matrix directions removes 35–59.5% of the 2M branch penalty for branches started between steps 1,000 and 9,000. A random subspace of the same size changes the penalty by at most 3%.

Protocol, controls, and what this does not establish

The held subspace is fixed at each anchor and comes from a Muon-preconditioned Gauss-Newton curvature proxy. Both Muon copies receive the same stream of base-batch gradients; the large-batch copy accumulates 16 of them before an update. Matrix weight decay and auxiliary AdamW updates stay on the base clock. This is a diagnostic intervention, not an ordinary large-batch training run.

Endpoints use 10.5M held-out tokens. Most branches are single runs, so these percentages are descriptive measurements, not significance estimates. The experiment selects directions by curvature and does not measure the full direction-dependent noise covariance. Its effect largely disappears near the end of training. We therefore cannot infer a 59.5% improvement in final loss, total training cost, or wall-clock speed.

The recovery share grows with the number of held directions, and the top 16 directions remove 5–26% of the local penalty. The effect disappears at the end of training: for branches started at steps 11,000 and 12,000, preserving a held subspace barely changes the penalty.

These results indicate that the diagnostic intervention using square-root learning-rate scaling produces different batch-size penalties across directions. The experiment selects directions by curvature and leaves gradient-noise anisotropy for future work. The measurements are local branch endpoints, mostly from single runs; they do not establish an end-to-end improvement in final loss or training cost. Future batch-size scaling methods should account for directional variation in CNR and explore how to exploit it.

Choose the optimizer and batch size together.

Our results challenge established practices in optimizer evaluation and design. Comparisons at a single batch size are not sufficient to establish the efficacy of an optimizer. We recommend extensive hyperparameter tuning across several batch sizes. The results here compare validation loss at a fixed token budget; wall-clock efficiency requires a separate comparison.

Optimizer design must account for how batch size changes the benefits and costs of adaptation. The noisy-quadratic analysis links scaling behavior to directional curvature-to-noise ratios. The language-model intervention shows that sharp directions carry much of the local batch-size penalty. How to exploit these directional differences in a practical scaling rule remains open.
